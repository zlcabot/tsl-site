title: Tending
description: The master verb — to stretch, and to care. The heart of this work.

Begin with the word. *Tend* comes down from an old root meaning **to
stretch** — the same root that gives us *tension*, *tendon*, *tent*.
Latin kept it as *tendere*, and then did something quietly brilliant:
it added directions. *Ex-tendere*, to stretch out. *In-tendere*, to
stretch into. *Ad-tendere*, to stretch toward. English inherited all
three: **extend, intend, attend**. Three directions of one verb.

And *tend* carries a second sense, from the same stretching: **to care
for**. A gardener tends. A shepherd tends. We tend a fire, tend the
sick, tend a friendship. Care is a stretching-toward that stays.

The claim at the heart of this work is that this word, taken seriously,
is a better guide to what things are than the vocabulary we usually
reach for. We talk about the world as if it were made of stuff —
objects that sit there, persisting, until something happens to them.
Look closer and the sitting-there dissolves: a stone, a cell, a
conversation, a person are each better read as **happenings that
stretch** — events, not substances — alive exactly as long as their
stretching continues, and held in being by nothing else.

Every happening stretches three ways at once:

- **[[extending]]** — stretching *out of* what has been, into this.
  The direction of inheritance and body: how everything that ever
  formed it lives on in the happening now.
- **[[intending]]** — stretching *with*. The relational direction: the
  holding-together of different things that stay different — the
  membrane, the between, the with.
- **[[attending]]** — stretching *toward*. The caring direction:
  directedness without a fixed destination, attention as a form of
  care.

These are not three parts, and not three ingredients that combine.
They are three directions of one stretch, and none can be reduced to
the others. Extending without attending is repetition going nowhere.
Attending without extending is direction with nothing formed enough to
move. Intending without both has nothing distinct to hold together.
Wherever there is a happening at all, all three are at work.

One more thing is true of every tending, and it may be the most
important: it happens [[the-once|once]]. No stretch repeats. What
completes does not come back — it becomes what later happenings stretch
out of.

This is offered as a way of reading, not a doctrine — as far as we can
tell, and revisable in public. The invitation is practical: assume it,
and tend. Read one thing near you — this page, your breath, the room —
as a stretching rather than a thing, and see what becomes sayable that
was not sayable before.
