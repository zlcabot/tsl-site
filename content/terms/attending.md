Attending is [[tending]] **toward** — care's direction. An attendant
is one who cares. A physician attends. We attend to a child, a guest,
a dying friend. Before attention became a resource to be spent, it was
a way of caring — the leaning of a happening toward what calls it. And
the toward is an act, not a schedule: it is happening now.

• • • —

## The word

*Ad-tendere*: to tend toward. English keeps the caring sense
everywhere it keeps this word — attendant, attending physician,
attention as something *paid* only in the late, spent-down usage.
The third way of [[tending]] restores the older grain: the toward as
care.

## What it names

Attending is directedness without a fixed destination. Not a plan,
not a goal, not a schedule — those are the toward frozen into borrowed
forms. It is the sunflower's slow turn. The ear finding the one voice
in a full room. The care that orients a whole life without ever being
written down. And it is not "the future": the toward is now —
direction is something an act does, not a place it is headed.

## The failure

Attending's characteristic failure is **fixation**: the toward seizes
one object and cannot release — the grip that mistakes itself for
care, the vigilance that has forgotten what it guards. Which reveals
the deep thing about attending's health: it is as much release as
reach. Real attention can let go. Care that cannot release its object
has stopped being care and become possession. The highest form of the
toward is an open one — directed, warm, unclenched.

## Relations

One of three ways of one act: without [[extending]] there is nothing
formed to lean toward anything, and without [[intending]] the toward
has no with to travel in. The whole is at [[tending]].
