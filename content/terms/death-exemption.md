Most bodies of thought keep one thing back. Not as a secret, and not as a lie. Usually it is an idea so useful and so cherished that looking straight at it comes to feel unnecessary, and then, after a while, more than impolite. It becomes the thing everything else is standing on, and so the thing nobody examines. That is a death exemption: the one item a tradition has excused from ending.

One question finds it, and it can be asked of anything.

**What here is not allowed to die?**

Death, in this question's sense, is wider than the biological kind. A cell dies. A habit stops being re-enacted. A theory is abandoned. A law stops holding. What the question looks for is whatever a body of work treats as not made, not maintained, not able to stop, or invariant at no cost. Biological death is one species of that, the one that makes the rest hard to ignore.

Run it and you find something almost everywhere. Biology's answer is the gene, not the molecule but the message, treated as simply carried while the bodies carrying it are spent. Physics has the law, which does not itself occur and so is not itself at risk. Psychology has traits. Mathematics has theorems, true before anyone was there to prove them. The contemplative traditions have their own and defend them well: awareness that was never born, the creator who was not made, the unconditioned that does not arise or pass. Yours is probably your self.

There are two sides to this, and the second is the sympathetic one. Every serious attempt to know anything is in part a search for what lasts, and that search is not an error. It is most of what knowledge is, and it has been rewarded beyond anyone's hopes. The exemption is what happens at the end of it: having found something that lasts remarkably well, we stop asking what the lasting costs, and a very good persistence gets quietly promoted into a permanent one.

This is a diagnostic, not a law. It is useful exactly as long as it keeps turning something up, and it makes no claim that every tradition must hide something from death. It is a tool. What makes it worth carrying is what happens next: refuse the exemption, assume the thing dies like everything else, and the picture reorganizes. Nothing the field found is lost. What changes is which of its facts get to be boring.

And the question is turnable on the one asking it. This work's own answer is that everything dies, around here, which is a claim made from inside a life and not from outside all of them, and which will end along with the rest. A diagnostic that exempted itself would be the very thing it is looking for.

The long form is the essay [[what-this-work-does|The death exemption: what can you not let die?]], where the question is run across the sciences and the traditions and then turned on the reader.
