Báyò Akómoláfé is a Nigerian-born psychologist, writer, and public thinker whose work joins Yoruba worlds, decolonial thought, ecopsychology, posthumanism, and process-relational philosophy. His recurring figures are cracks, monsters, fugitivity, fermentation, exile, and sanctuary. These are not decorative metaphors added to a conventional political program. They unsettle the world, agent, and future that a program ordinarily presumes.

Akómoláfé matters here because he asks a question that reform and revolution can share an interest in avoiding: what if the way we identify and solve a problem is one of the processes through which the problem persists?

## The middle and the crack

_These Wilds Beyond Our Fences_ is written as letters to Akómoláfé's daughter. The form is philosophically important. He does not offer a view from above history. He writes as a son, father, Nigerian, traveler, psychologist, and inheritor of Christianity and colonial modernity. The inquiry changes the writer's availability to the relationship in which he writes.

The book contests beginnings, destinations, and pristine restoration. We are always already in the middle of worlding. A crack is therefore not merely damage to an otherwise intact surface. It can disclose that the surface was never complete, release actors excluded by its order, and make a simple return impossible. Repair becomes re-turn: an encounter with what the break has made newly possible rather than a restoration of innocence.[^1]

This account has an unusual temporal force. The middle is not the interval between a secure origin and a promised destination. It is where inheritance, disruption, and unfinished emergence are active together. Akómoláfé's monsters inhabit that middle. They mark forms of life and agency that a settled map had to render unthinkable.

## Postactivism and slower urgency

[[postactivism]] names the political consequence. It does not mean that activism has ended, that injustice should be tolerated, or that withdrawal is inherently wise. The “post” marks an interruption in which the inherited arrangement of problem, solver, solution, and success no longer fits.

In his concise account of the term, Akómoláfé describes postactivism through fissures, discontinuities, strange encounters, and world-ending openings. It is not a superior method or a guaranteed route to utopia. It occurs where continuity becomes impossible and where more-than-human participation disrupts the exclusivity of human agency.[^2]

His invitation, “the times are urgent; let us slow down,” should be read inside this argument.[^3] Slowing is not a universal preference for low velocity. It interrupts the reflex by which urgency narrows attention to familiar experts, scales, tools, and outcomes. To slow down is to notice how the response participates in the field, what it has made invisible, and what other actors are already doing.

Sanctuary, in this register, is not a protected enclosure outside crisis. It is a practice of composing with grief, decomposition, fugitivity, and strangeness when the old coordinates no longer organize an adequate response.

## Where this work meets Akómoláfé

Akómoláfé is one of the closest contemporary neighbors to reflexive temporal [[tending]]. He refuses the sovereign actor who stands outside a passive world and imposes change upon it. The responder is implicated in the event and can be altered by responding. His cracks also give practical and political force to the axiom that description is making: the frame of a problem helps constitute the actors, choices, and futures that become available.

The convergence becomes especially close around reflexivity. A response can include its own habits and effects among what it responds to. It can notice that its urgency, moral reward, institutional position, and image of success are themselves happenings in the field. This is not paralysis. It is agency becoming answerable to its own agency.

Yet tending makes a different temporal claim. Akómoláfé often gives the crack, interruption, slowness, and failure privileged rhetorical force. Tending treats none as intrinsically emancipatory. Every act extends, intends, and attends in a temporicity of its own. The practical question is what keeps time with what, which timing a response interrupts, who bears the mismatch, and what forms of completion or renewal the intervention permits.

## The harder problem: direct harm

Postactivism is strongest when inherited action repeatedly reproduces what it opposes. It is most vulnerable when delay itself is an instrument of power.

A person facing police violence, expulsion, domestic abuse, medical emergency, or ecological destruction may need an immediate and forceful intervention. Institutions frequently request more dialogue, more study, or more patience from people already carrying the harm. In such cases, slowing down can protect the dominant timetable rather than interrupt it.

This does not refute Akómoláfé. It makes the location of the slowing decisive. A rapid protective act may interrupt the slow violence of an institution. A pause imposed on the powerful may create time for those denied it. A pause imposed on the endangered may become abandonment.

Distributed agency also cannot mean equal responsibility. A river, a weapon, a bureaucracy, a frightened witness, and an armed official may all participate in an event without possessing the same alternatives or obligations. More-than-human agency widens the field of causation. It must not flatten political answerability.

## What Akómoláfé changes here

Akómoláfé prevents tending from being presented as a better control system. Reflexivity does not give the agent a more complete cockpit. It can expose the cockpit as one of the arrangements that has to crack. He also makes failure more than negative evidence. An intervention can fail generatively if its consequences change the responder's future capacity, while the harm of the failure remains real.

The unresolved test moves in both directions. Postactivism must show how it distinguishes interruption from abdication under conditions of direct harm. Tending must show that its language of temporal fit can be enacted without becoming a refined vocabulary for managerial judgment. Neither can answer from outside the scene.

## Sources and ways in

_These Wilds Beyond Our Fences_ is the best entrance to Akómoláfé's larger imaginative and philosophical world. “What I Mean by Postactivism” gives the clearest short definition of the political term. “A Slower Urgency” places the invitation to slow down within more-than-human participation rather than lifestyle minimalism.
