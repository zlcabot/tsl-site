Hospicing modernity is [[vanessa-machado-de-oliveira|Vanessa Machado de Oliveira's]] name for care at a civilizational ending. The phrase does not mean causing modernity's death, celebrating collapse, or withdrawing from damaged institutions. It asks what care becomes when indefinite continuation is no longer the only good. It also asks whether people formed by modernity can accompany its ending without turning hospice into another project of control.

• • • —

## What is being hospiced

Modernity is not one object with one boundary. Machado de Oliveira uses the word for a colonial formation joining conquest, racial hierarchy, extraction, property, accumulation, human supremacy, universal reason, and the promise of unrestricted autonomy. It persists through laws and economies, but also through desires for certainty, innocence, moral authority, speed, scale, and solutions that displace their costs.[^1]

This makes the patient difficult to locate. Modernity is not simply outside its critics. It can organize how criticism feels, which futures appear serious, and what kinds of action provide moral satisfaction. The demand to fix everything quickly may reproduce the same authority and universality the intervention opposes.

The metaphor should therefore remain plural and situated. A public health institution, a fossil-fuel economy, a system of racial extraction, a constitutional right, and a person's desire for innocence do not share one prognosis. Some arrangements need defense, some repair, some transformation, and some completion. “Modernity is dying” cannot be allowed to decide those cases in advance.

## What hospice means

Machado de Oliveira describes hospicing as assistance to systems that are dying and support for relinquishment where people cling to what is already gone.[^2] The clinical analogy gives the practice four commitments.

First, hospice does not confuse care with cure. Second, it does not abandon the dying because cure is unavailable. Third, it does not treat continued biological function as the only measure of a good outcome. Fourth, it attends to pain, relation, unfinished obligation, and the people whose lives are organized around the ending.

Applied to modernity, those commitments require capacities that ordinary political strategy often treats as secondary. Grief matters. So do complicity, uncertainty, failure, loss of status, and the inability to promise that sacrifice will produce a better world. Machado de Oliveira calls this preparation “depth education.” Its task is to make responsibility active before a sovereign will imagines itself in charge.[^3]

## Hospice is not passivity

The metaphor is frequently heard as an invitation to sit beside collapse. The book itself is more demanding.

Hospicing includes disinvestment from desires and reflexes that keep destructive arrangements alive. It includes mapping harms, differentiating responsibility, interrupting compulsive intervention, redistributing burdens, repairing relations, supporting people at unequal risk, and learning from failed gestures. Machado de Oliveira's police encounter is a concrete example. Witnessing the same event does not assign everyone the same action because bodies carry different exposure to state violence.[^4]

This is one reason hospicing should not be grouped automatically with contemplative quietism or terminal realism. The practice refuses heroic rescue. It does not refuse action. Its constructive work begins with the capacity and relation from which action is made.

## What hospice makes visible

Hospice exposes a bias hidden in many theories of care: the assumption that care always means extending the form already present.

A relationship may be sustained beyond the point at which either person can live truthfully inside it. An institution may preserve its procedures while defeating its purpose. An economy may call its own continuation stability while making more places uninhabitable. A movement may keep a strategy alive because ending it would feel like betrayal.

Hospicing asks a different question. What would fidelity require if continuation were no longer presumed?

That question matters for [[tending]]. Tending is often mistaken for maintenance because gardens, bodies, institutions, and relationships need repeated care. Hospice reveals another form of tending. Sometimes care supports a happening toward completion. The work is not less active because the form will not survive.

## The danger of one patient

The civilizational metaphor also carries risks.

Modernity has not given one world to everyone. Colonial conquest and ecological destruction have already ended worlds, while some of their beneficiaries are only beginning to experience instability. A universal deathbed can center the grief of those losing privilege and obscure the people who have long practiced survival, refusal, and reconstruction after imposed endings.[^5]

The metaphor may also collapse unlike problems into one diagnosis. Medicine, rights, bureaucracy, extraction, racial capitalism, individualism, and scientific method become features of a single dying organism. Once gathered this way, reforms can be dismissed as life support even where they materially reduce harm.

A responsible use of hospicing therefore needs local prognosis. Who or what is dying? For whom? Over what while? Which relations are being protected? Who bears the symptoms, and who benefits from declaring the patient beyond repair?

## Death and birth

Machado de Oliveira pairs hospicing with midwifery. Her writing often moves through death, clearing, liminality, composting, gestation, and birth. The new must not be suffocated by a projection supplied by the old.[^6]

This is constructive and non-blueprint, but it still gives the ending a regenerative sequence. A larger metabolism survives the transition and supports another form.

The reading here holds the relation between death and novelty more open. An ending does not guarantee a birth. It does not need a successor in order to have completed. What happened will condition other happenings, but neither its value nor its reality depends on a larger cycle turning loss into renewal.

This distinction protects hospice from covert optimism. It also protects it from despair. If death guarantees neither catastrophe nor rebirth, attention returns to the actual conditions being tended now.

## What tending adds

[[Tending]] keeps three parts of the hospice insight.

- Care is not synonymous with preservation.
- The responder's own formation belongs inside the work.
- Construction without guarantee can begin through conditions, capacities, and relations before a successor form is known.

It adds several cautions.

- Modernity completes differently across institutions, peoples, ecologies, and practices. There is no single hospice clock.
- Relation does not require one patient, one metabolism, or one civilizational subject.
- Responsibility follows actual implication, capacity, harm, and consequence. It can be radical without becoming unlimited.
- Completion is not closure. A form can finish while its consequences remain active and while other possibilities stay open.
- Novelty is not the reward supplied by death. It arrives without guarantee.

## What hospicing changes here

The encounter corrects any account of tending that treats continuation as its native success condition. It also forces the work to address disinvestment. If a pattern is sustained by pleasure, status, innocence, convenience, or institutional reward, explaining the pattern will not be enough to end it.

Hospicing leaves a harder demand. Can tending help build practices and institutions capable of relinquishment without making decline into an identity? Can it defend what remains emancipatory inside modernity while helping destructive forms complete? Those questions stay open by design.

## Sources and ways in

The term is developed most fully in _Hospicing Modernity_. _Outgrowing Modernity_ shows how the metaphor grows into an explicit practice of tethers, relational maturity, and construction after disinvestment.
