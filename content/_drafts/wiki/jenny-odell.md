Jenny Odell is an American artist and writer whose work moves among ecology, technology, labor, attention, and public space. _How to Do Nothing_ examines refusal without withdrawal from the world. _Saving Time_ turns from captured attention to the historical production of clock time, temporal scarcity, and the unequal power to decide whose time matters.

Odell's importance here lies in the passage she makes between ordinary experience and temporal philosophy. Burnout, waiting, a work schedule, a flowering plant, a prison sentence, and a climate deadline become evidence that time is neither one neutral substance nor merely a private feeling.

## The clock as a regime

Odell does not argue that clocks are unreal or useless. Standardized time coordinates trains, meetings, experiments, and collective work. Her argument is historical and political: one local technology of coordination became a dominant language for value.

_Saving Time_ connects measured time to commerce, colonial administration, industrial labor, and extraction. Time becomes homogeneous stuff that can be divided, purchased, optimized, and lost. Yet the same hour does not belong equally to an employer and an employee, a prisoner and a guard, a caregiver and a client, or a person whose schedule others are expected to absorb.[^1]

The problem is therefore not measurement alone. It is the promotion of measurement into an account of what time is and what a life is worth.

## More than one time

Under the timetable, Odell observes pregnancy, healing, memory, desire, weather, dormancy, migration, geological formation, and ecological succession. Their rhythms overlap without becoming interchangeable. This can produce the suspicion that we are “living on the wrong clock.”[^2]

Her term _chronodiversity_ names the plurality of tempos and temporal forms already active in social and ecological life. A botanical garden is not only biodiverse. It brings different speeds of growth, flowering, decay, work, ritual, and visitation into relation. Odell asks whether time could be gardened by protecting and composing such rhythms instead of being saved and spent as a scarce commodity.[^3]

This is not an escape from politics into nature. Ecological time has been disturbed by extraction, climate change, settler histories, and uneven exposure. Bodies and communities also inhabit different time regimes because power distributes waiting, flexibility, urgency, and futurity unequally.

## Attention as participation

Odell's earlier work makes the connection to attention. Attention is not simply a private resource stolen by devices. What a person learns to notice changes the world that can address them. Birds, transit systems, neighborhoods, labor histories, and damaged landscapes disclose relations that the optimized schedule renders peripheral.

Attention is therefore material and political. It can restore no pristine world, but it can alter which happenings solicit response and which forms of coordination become imaginable. Refusal-in-place means remaining answerable to a place while declining the demand that every perception become productive output.[^4]

## Where this work meets Odell

Odell gives a public doorway into the claim that a happening does not merely occupy an amount of time. Its happening is its time. A moss, a strike, a grief, and a storm are not four objects moving through the same temporal container. Each is a temporicity with its own inheritance, tensions, arc, and completion.

Shared time then becomes an accomplishment. Happenings keep time together when their rhythms become mutually consequential. A schedule is one way of accomplishing this, but so are a season, a ritual, a conversation, a watershed, and a body responding to another body.

This is where [[tending]] extends Odell's insight. Chronodiversity identifies plural rhythms. Tending asks how each act extends out of completed acts, intends with others in a shared while, and attends toward its own completion. [[consciousness|Consciousness]] adds the reflexive fold through which an act can include its clock, urgency, and habits of attention among what it tends.

## The pressure points

Three cautions matter.

First, ecological time cannot become an innocent outside to social time. Ecosystems contain disturbance, predation, extinction, engineering, and inherited harm. No natural rhythm settles a political question simply by being natural.

Second, attention cannot become a private cure for structural domination. A worker may perceive multiple temporalities and still lack control over a shift. A prison controls time through force. A caregiver's flexibility may be someone else's unpaid burden. Temporal plurality requires institutional arrangements, not only perceptual awakening.

Third, not every clock is a master clock. Shared measures can protect people, coordinate care, and make exploitation visible. The question is which timing has been universalized, what work it does, whose temporicities it suppresses, and whether those governed by it can revise it.

## What Odell changes here

Odell forces tending to remain publicly legible and materially located. A philosophy of temporicity must be able to speak about wages, schedules, incarceration, disability, care, land, and climate without reducing them to illustrations of an ontology.

She also sharpens the difference between temporal plurality and relativism. Rhythms do not float in private worlds. They meet, coordinate, constrain, nourish, and injure one another. Their conflicts are real precisely because no universal clock dissolves their differences.

## Sources and ways in

_Saving Time_ is the primary entrance for clock time, chronodiversity, ecological timing, and temporal justice. _How to Do Nothing_ is the stronger beginning for attention, refusal-in-place, and the politics of noticing.
